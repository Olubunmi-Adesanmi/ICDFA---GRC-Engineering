## 📌 Project Overview

This repository presents my completed **GRC102 Information Security Governance** practical laboratory, based on the fictional **GlobalHealth Connect (GHC)** executive governance simulation.

The simulation placed me in the role of **Director of Information Security Governance** for a growing health-technology organisation that provides cloud-based patient-management systems. Following rapid growth, two acquisitions, and a contained data-leakage near-miss, GHC needed to move from an informal and reactive security approach to a structured, accountable, and business-aligned information security governance programme.

This project demonstrates how effective governance connects:

- Business strategy
- Enterprise risk management
- Executive accountability
- Regulatory considerations
- Security metrics
- Board reporting
- Cross-functional decision-making
- Governance maturity and continuous improvement

> **Key takeaway:** Effective information security governance is not about assigning security to one department. It is about ensuring that security risks are understood, owned, communicated, and managed at the appropriate level of the organisation.

## 🔍 Governance Diagnosis

### Current-State Assessment

GHC's security environment was assessed as **ad hoc and reactive**.

Key weaknesses included:

- Unclear security ownership.
- Lack of formal enterprise security accountability.
- Limited Board-level security reporting.
- Inconsistent policies inherited from acquisitions.
- Reactive rather than proactive risk management.
- Weak separation between governance and operational security activities.
- Technical metrics that were not consistently translated into business impact.
- Compliance practices driven primarily by audits.
- Low security-awareness engagement.
- Insufficient mechanisms for resolving cross-functional security decisions.


### Root-Cause Finding

The primary problem was not the absence of security activity. The problem was that existing activities were **fragmented across departments and lacked a common governance framework**.

The proposed solution therefore connected:

```text
Accountability
      ↓
Risk Ownership
      ↓
Decision Rights
      ↓
Reporting
      ↓
Assurance
      ↓
Continuous Improvement
```

## 📊 Board Security Metrics

### Six-Month Trend Analysis

| Metric | September | February | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **Phishing Events** | 150 | 320 | **+113%** |
| **Malware Events** | 25 | 50 | **+100%** |
| **Critical Vulnerability Patching** | 67% | 55% | **-12 percentage points** |
| **Security Training Completion** | 45% | 70% | **+25 percentage points** |
| **High-Risk Incidents** | 2 | 7 | **+250%** |

### Overall Board-Level Posture

## 🔴 RED: Immediate Management Attention Required

The rating reflects deterioration across four major risk indicators:

- Phishing events increased substantially.
- Malware events doubled.
- High-risk incidents increased by 250%.
- Critical vulnerability patching coverage declined.

Training completion improved, but this positive movement did not offset the broader deterioration in security exposure.

### Key Analytical Finding

> **More vulnerabilities patched does not necessarily mean better vulnerability management.**

GHC patched:

- **10 of 15** critical vulnerabilities in September.
- **22 of 40** critical vulnerabilities in February.

Although the number patched increased from 10 to 22, coverage declined from **67% to 55%**. Remediation activity was therefore not keeping pace with the expanding critical-vulnerability backlog.

This demonstrates why activity metrics must be evaluated alongside exposure, coverage, and business risk.

---

## ⚠️ Priority Security Risks

### 1. Increasing Security Incident Exposure

**Evidence:** Phishing increased by 113%, malware increased by 100%, and high-risk incidents increased by 250%.

**Potential business impact:**

- Patient-information exposure
- Operational disruption
- Regulatory consequences
- Reputational damage
- Customer loss
- Increased incident-response costs

**Recommended response:** Implement a focused threat-exposure reduction programme covering MFA, identity and access management, endpoint security, phishing resistance, security awareness, and incident-response readiness.

### 2. Declining Critical Vulnerability Coverage

**Evidence:** Critical vulnerability patching coverage declined from 67% to 55%.

**Recommended response:** Establish risk-based vulnerability-remediation service-level agreements prioritised by severity, exploitability, asset criticality, exposure, and business impact. Critical exceptions should require documented risk acceptance.

### 3. Governance Accountability Gap

**Evidence:** Security ownership was unclear, while risk management remained largely reactive.

**Recommended response:** Establish an Information Security Charter, Security Steering Committee, security RACI, enterprise security-risk register, and formal risk-acceptance process.

## 🔐 Password Policy Governance Case

GHC considered a proposed password policy requiring:

> 16-character complex passwords, password changes every 30 days, and no password-manager integration.

The proposal created a conflict between stronger control expectations and concerns about usability and developer productivity.

### Governance Decision

The policy should **not be adopted in its original form**.

A more balanced authentication approach was recommended using:

- Multi-factor authentication
- Strong passwords or passphrases
- Password-manager support
- Compromised-password detection, where feasible
- - Password changes following suspected or confirmed compromise
- Privileged-access controls
- Authentication monitoring

This recommendation balances security effectiveness, usability, and operational productivity.
