## Executive Summary

GlobalHealth Connect (GHC) is a fictional mid-sized health-technology organisation that provides cloud-based patient-management systems. The organisation has experienced rapid growth, completed two acquisitions, and is pursuing new features and expansion into two regional markets. A contained data-leakage near-miss exposed weaknesses in the organisation's information security governance arrangements.

The assessment found that GHC's security activities were not absent, but were fragmented across departments. Security ownership was unclear, inherited policies were inconsistent, risk management was reactive, Board reporting was limited, and technical metrics were not consistently translated into business impact. Governance and operational responsibilities were also insufficiently separated.

The current governance maturity was assessed at Level 1 to Level 2 across six domains: policy and documentation, roles and responsibilities, risk management, metrics and reporting, training and awareness, and compliance. The most serious weaknesses were accountability and risk management.

A formal governance model was therefore designed around six connected elements:

Accountability → Risk Ownership → Decision Rights → Reporting → Assurance → Continuous Improvement

The proposed model establishes Board oversight, CEO accountability, a Director of Information Security Governance, and a cross-functional Security Steering Committee. It also introduces a security RACI, an Information Security Charter, an enterprise security-risk register, a formal risk-acceptance process, quarterly Board reporting, and defined governance performance measures.

The six-month security data supports a RED Board-level posture. Phishing events increased from 150 to 320, malware events increased from 25 to 50, high-risk incidents increased from 2 to 7, and critical vulnerability patching coverage declined from 67% to 55%. Security training completion improved from 45% to 70%, but this improvement did not offset the deterioration in the other risk indicators.

A 12–18 month roadmap was developed to move all six governance domains to at least Level 3 – Defined. The roadmap prioritises governance and accountability, enterprise risk management, policy and control standardisation, metrics and assurance, security culture, and incident readiness.

The proposed programme directly supports GHC's strategic priorities by enabling secure growth, reducing reactive work, protecting patient information, supporting innovation through security by design, and moving compliance from an audit-driven activity to a continuous governance process.

## Task 1: Governance Blueprint

### 7.1 CURRENT-STATE GOVERNANCE GAP ASSESSMENT

GHC's current security environment was assessed as ad hoc and reactive.

Gap 1: Unclear Security Ownership

Security responsibilities were distributed across departments without clear enterprise accountability. This creates a risk that important decisions may be delayed, duplicated, or left unresolved.

Gap 2: Limited Board-Level Oversight

Security information was not consistently presented to the Board in a form that connected technical conditions to business exposure, strategic objectives, and risk-treatment decisions.

Gap 3: Inconsistent Policies

The two acquisitions introduced inherited policies and practices that were not fully reconciled. This creates uncertainty, uneven control implementation, and inconsistent expectations across the organisation.

Gap 4: Reactive Risk Management

Risk activity was driven mainly by incidents, near misses, and audits. The organisation lacked a consistent enterprise security-risk register, formal risk ownership, and a defined treatment and acceptance process.

Gap 5: Weak Separation of Governance and Operations

Governance and operational security activities were not clearly separated. A governance function should establish direction, oversight, policy, decision rights, and assurance, while operational teams implement and maintain controls.

Gap 6: Technical Rather Than Business-Focused Reporting

Technical measures existed, but they were not consistently translated into exposure, service impact, customer trust, regulatory consequences, or required management decisions.

Gap 7: Audit-Driven Compliance

Compliance activity was focused mainly on preparation for audits rather than continuous monitoring and sustained control effectiveness.

Gap 8: Low Security-Awareness Engagement

Training was mandatory, but engagement and completion were insufficient. Awareness activity also needed to be linked to behavioural risks such as phishing.

Gap 9: Weak Cross-Functional Decision Mechanisms

GHC did not have a sufficiently formal forum for resolving decisions that affected security, technology, finance, compliance, operations, human resources, and business delivery.

### 7.2 ROOT-CAUSE DIAGNOSIS

The central issue was not that GHC had no security activities. The core problem was that existing activities were fragmented and lacked a common governance framework.

The proposed governance response connects:

- Accountability: Who ultimately owns the decision or outcome?
- Risk Ownership: Which business owner is responsible for managing each material risk?
- Decision Rights: Who may approve policy, treatment, exceptions, or risk acceptance?
- Reporting: What information must be communicated, to whom, and at what frequency?
- Assurance: How will management and the Board know that controls and decisions are effective?
- Continuous Improvement: How will incidents, metrics, reviews, and lessons learned drive improvement?

### 7.4 ROLES AND REPORTING RELATIONSHIPS

#### Board of Directors

- Provides oversight of material information security risk.
- Reviews the organisation's security posture and major risk decisions.
- Challenges management where exposure exceeds risk appetite.
- Receives regular business-focused security reporting.

#### Chief Executive Officer

- Holds executive accountability for the effectiveness of the governance programme.
- Ensures security risk is integrated into business decision-making.
- Sponsors the Security Steering Committee.
- Escalates material matters to the Board.

#### Director of Information Security Governance

- Coordinates the governance framework.
- Maintains governance artefacts and reporting processes.
- Supports risk identification, ownership, treatment, and escalation.
- Produces executive and Board-level security reporting.
- Monitors governance maturity and improvement actions.
- Provides governance oversight without assuming responsibility for every operational control.

#### Security Steering Committee

- Provides cross-functional review and decision-making.
- Reviews material risks, policies, exceptions, incidents, metrics, and strategic initiatives.
- Resolves conflicts between security requirements and business priorities.
- Escalates matters beyond delegated authority.

#### Operational and Business Functions

- Implement and maintain controls within their areas.
- Own relevant systems, processes, data, and business risks.
- Provide evidence and metrics.
- Escalate material issues and exceptions.

### 7.6 GOVERNANCE RATIONALE

The model establishes oversight at the Board level, accountability at the executive level, coordination through an independent governance role, and implementation through operational functions.

It deliberately avoids making the governance function responsible for every control. If governance both directs and performs all control activity, independent challenge and assurance become weaker. The design therefore applies the principle:

“Governance defines direction, oversight, and accountability; operational functions implement and maintain controls.”

The Security Steering Committee creates a formal mechanism for decisions that cross functional boundaries. The RACI reduces ambiguity by identifying one accountable role for important governance outcomes.

## Task 2: Information Security Charter

### 8.1 GHC INFORMATION SECURITY CHARTER

1. Purpose

The purpose of this Charter is to establish the mandate, authority, governance principles, responsibilities, and reporting arrangements for GHC's information security programme.

2. Objectives

The information security programme will:

- Protect the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information and services.
- Support GHC's strategic and operational objectives.
- Ensure that material security risks are identified, owned, treated, monitored, and reported.
- Support appropriate protection of patient and organisational information.
- Enable secure innovation and growth.
- Promote compliance with applicable obligations.
- Strengthen resilience and continuous improvement.

3. Scope

This Charter applies to:

- All GHC business units and functions.
- Employees, contractors, temporary workers, and relevant third parties.
- Information in physical, digital, or other forms.
- Technology, cloud services, applications, infrastructure, and endpoints.
- Business processes and services.
- Acquired entities and integrated operations.
- Third parties that process, store, transmit, or access GHC information.

4. Authority

The information security governance function is authorised to:

- Develop and maintain security governance policies and standards.
- Coordinate enterprise security-risk assessments.
- Request information and evidence needed for governance reporting.
- Escalate material security risks, exceptions, and control weaknesses.
- Facilitate the Security Steering Committee.
- Provide security-risk reporting to executive management and the Board.
- Recommend corrective actions and governance improvements.

The governance function does not replace the responsibility of business and operational owners to implement controls and manage risk within their areas.

5. Governance Principles

GHC adopts the following principles:

1. Risk-based decision-making
2. Business alignment
3. Clear accountability
4. Proportionate security controls
5. Security and privacy by design
6. Least privilege
7. Defence in depth
8. Continuous improvement
9. Evidence-based assurance
10. Appropriate protection of sensitive information

6. Roles and Responsibilities

#### Board of Directors

The Board oversees material information security risk and receives regular reporting on posture, trends, major incidents, risk treatment, and governance maturity.

#### Chief Executive Officer

The CEO is accountable for ensuring that the governance programme is supported, integrated into business decision-making, and appropriately resourced.

#### Director of Information Security Governance

The Director coordinates the governance framework, facilitates risk reporting, maintains governance artefacts, and monitors improvement.

#### Security Steering Committee

The Committee reviews material security matters, supports cross-functional decisions, monitors treatment progress, and escalates issues where required.

#### Business and Risk Owners

Business owners are accountable for risks, systems, processes, information, and decisions within their authority. They must ensure appropriate treatment or formally approved acceptance.

#### Operational Functions

Operational functions are responsible for implementing, operating, monitoring, and providing evidence for controls assigned to them.

#### All Personnel

All personnel must comply with approved policies, complete required awareness activities, protect GHC information, and report suspected incidents or weaknesses.

7. Risk Management

GHC will maintain a documented security-risk process covering identification, assessment, ownership, treatment, acceptance, monitoring, review, and escalation.

Material risks must have named owners. Critical risks must have approved treatment plans or documented acceptance by the appropriate authority.

8. Policy Governance

Critical security policies must have:

- A defined owner.
- Executive or delegated approval.
- A documented review cycle.
- Version control.
- Communication and implementation arrangements.
- A controlled exception process.

9. Reporting

Security reporting will include relevant key risk indicators, key performance indicators, trend analysis, material incidents, treatment progress, policy exceptions, and matters requiring decisions.

Board security reporting will occur quarterly, with urgent escalation for material events.

10. Review and Approval

This Charter will be reviewed at least annually or following significant organisational, regulatory, technological, or risk changes.

Approval Authority: Chief Executive Officer and Board of Directors
Review Owner: Director of Information Security Governance
Review Frequency: Annual

### 8.2 CFO JUSTIFICATION MEMO

To: Chief Financial Officer
From: Director of Information Security Governance
Subject: Business Justification for the GHC Information Security Charter

The proposed Information Security Charter is required to provide GHC's security programme with a formal executive mandate and establish clear accountability for security-risk decisions.

The Charter does not make the governance function responsible for implementing every security control. Instead, it clarifies ownership, decision rights, reporting, and escalation. Operational teams remain responsible for controls within their areas, while business owners remain accountable for relevant risks.

The Charter supports financial and operational discipline in five ways:

1. It reduces duplicated and fragmented security activity by providing a common governance framework.
2. It supports risk-based prioritisation so that resources can be directed toward the most material exposures.
3. It formalises risk acceptance, reducing the chance that unresolved risk remains invisible.
4. It establishes measurable reporting so that investment decisions can be connected to business outcomes.
5. It supports acquisition integration by providing consistent expectations across GHC.

The contained data-leakage near-miss and deterioration in security indicators demonstrate that informal governance is no longer appropriate for the organisation's growth and risk profile.

Recommendation: Support approval of the Information Security Charter and the phased governance roadmap, with progress and resource requirements reviewed through the Security Steering Committee.

#### Phishing Events

Phishing events increased from 150 to 320. This indicates a substantial rise in exposure to social-engineering activity and supports prioritising stronger identity controls, phishing resistance, and targeted awareness.

#### Malware Events

Malware events increased from 25 to 50. The doubling of recorded events may indicate growing endpoint or user exposure and requires focused investigation and control review.

#### Critical Vulnerability Patching

Critical vulnerability patching coverage declined from 67% to 55%.

In September, GHC patched 10 of 15 critical vulnerabilities. In February, it patched 22 of 40. Although the absolute number patched increased, the total critical-vulnerability workload grew faster, causing the coverage rate to decline.

This demonstrates that an activity count, such as the number patched, can be misleading unless assessed against total exposure and risk.

#### Security Training Completion

Training completion improved from 45% to 70%. This is meaningful progress, but 30% remained incomplete in February. Completion alone also does not demonstrate behavioural effectiveness. The governance programme should therefore combine completion data with phishing simulation outcomes and repeat-risk indicators.

#### High-Risk Incidents

High-risk incidents increased from 2 to 7, representing the most severe proportional deterioration in the data. This requires management attention because such incidents may create operational, financial, compliance, and reputational consequences.

### 9.3 OVERALL SECURITY POSTURE

**Rating:** RED

Rationale:

- Four of five major indicators show deterioration or inadequate coverage.
- Threat activity increased substantially.
- High-risk incidents increased materially.
- Critical vulnerability coverage moved in the wrong direction.
- Governance weaknesses reduce confidence that risk decisions are consistently owned and monitored.

The RED rating does not mean that every security activity failed. It means that the combined direction of the indicators requires immediate management attention and structured risk treatment.

### 10.3 PASSWORD POLICY GOVERNANCE ANALYSIS

#### Policy Proposed

- Minimum 16-character complex passwords.
- Password changes every 30 days.
- No password-manager integration.

#### Stakeholder Positions

The IT Manager supported stronger controls. The CTO raised concerns that the policy would create unnecessary friction and reduce developer productivity.

#### Governance Assessment

The policy should not be approved in its original form.

A governance decision should evaluate actual risk reduction, usability, operational impact, and alignment with recognised guidance. A control that creates excessive friction may encourage predictable passwords, insecure workarounds, password reuse, or increased support activity.

#### Recommended Authentication Approach

- Multi-factor authentication.
- Strong passwords or passphrases.
- Password-manager support.
- Compromised-password detection where feasible.
- Password changes following suspected or confirmed compromise.
- Privileged-access controls.
- Authentication monitoring.

#### Decision

Reject the original proposal and instruct the relevant teams to develop a risk-based authentication standard using the recommended principles. The standard should define stronger requirements for privileged and higher-risk access and include a controlled exception process.

#### Rationale

The revised approach provides stronger overall protection while balancing security effectiveness, user experience, developer productivity, and maintainability.

### 10.4 CEO BRIEFING NOTE

To: Chief Executive Officer
From: Director of Information Security Governance
Subject: Decision on Proposed Password Policy

#### Issue

The proposed password policy requires 16-character complex passwords, changes every 30 days, and prohibits password-manager integration. The proposal has created disagreement between the CTO and IT Manager.

#### Assessment

The proposal appears strong because it imposes demanding password rules. However, arbitrary frequent changes and the lack of password-manager support could create user friction and encourage unsafe workarounds. Password controls should form part of a broader authentication approach rather than operating alone.

#### Recommendation

Do not approve the policy in its current form. Direct the Security Steering Committee to oversee a revised standard based on MFA, strong passphrases, password-manager support, compromised-password detection, changes following suspected or confirmed compromise, privileged-access controls, and authentication monitoring.

#### Business Benefit

The recommendation improves protection while reducing unnecessary friction and supporting developer productivity. It also demonstrates that GHC's security decisions are risk-based, evidence-informed, and aligned with business needs.

## Task 5: Governance Maturity

### 11.1 MATURITY ASSESSMENT

#### Maturity Scale

Level 1 – Ad Hoc: Activities are informal, inconsistent, and reactive.
Level 2 – Initial: Some repeatable practices exist, but they are incomplete or inconsistently applied.
Level 3 – Defined: Practices are documented, approved, assigned, and consistently implemented.
Level 4 – Managed: Performance is measured, monitored, and actively managed.
Level 5 – Optimised: Governance is continuously improved using evidence, automation, and lessons learned.

Domain 1: Policy and Documentation
**Current Level:** 2 – Initial

## Key Learning Outcomes

This practical laboratory strengthened my understanding of how Governance, Risk, and Compliance functions operate beyond documentation and compliance checklists.

A central learning point was that technical activity must not be confused with risk reduction. For example, GHC patched more critical vulnerabilities in February than in September, but remediation coverage still declined because the total number of critical vulnerabilities grew faster.

A second learning point was that governance must clarify who is accountable without transferring every operational responsibility to the governance function.

A third learning point was that effective Board reporting must translate technical evidence into business exposure, trends, ownership, treatment status, and decisions required.

## Conclusion

GHC's primary information security challenge is a governance problem rather than simply a lack of technical activity. Existing security efforts are fragmented, accountability is unclear, risk management is reactive, and executive reporting is not sufficiently business-focused.

The proposed governance programme creates a structured connection between Board oversight, executive accountability, cross-functional decision-making, risk ownership, operational implementation, reporting, and assurance.

The RED security posture requires immediate management attention. The most urgent needs are to reduce threat exposure, improve critical vulnerability coverage, resolve the governance accountability gap, and formalise risk treatment and acceptance.

The 12–18 month roadmap provides a measurable path from Level 1–2 maturity to Level 3 – Defined across all six domains. If implemented consistently, the programme will help GHC support secure growth, operational efficiency, customer trust, innovation, regulatory readiness, and continuous improvement.

Effective information security governance is not about making security the responsibility of one department. It is about ensuring that security risks are understood, owned, communicated, and managed at the right level of the organisation.
